# Lab 11: Mock Interview — Model Governance & Compliance

**Role**: ML Platform Engineer / ML Governance Engineer
**Duration**: 60 minutes
**Focus**: Model cards, fairness metrics (demographic parity, equal opportunity, disparate impact), hash-chained audit trails, compliance reporting

---

**Interviewer**: "Start with the model card. What did you put on it, and why does that specific set of fields exist?"

**Candidate**: "The lab's `ModelCard` captures the six sections regulators and reviewers actually read: identity — `modelName`, `version`, `modelType`, `framework`; provenance — the creation date; `intendedUse` — the contract of what the model is *for*, which is also what it must not be used for; `limitations` — the lab adds three, including 'training data skewed towards urban populations', which is the honesty section; and the two performance blocks — `perGroupMetrics` with accuracy/precision/recall/f1 plus a sample size per group, and `fairnessMetrics` when the bias detector has been run. The per-group block is the heart of it: 'Group B (Protected)' at 0.885 accuracy vs 'Group A (Majority)' at 0.935 — the card forces the team to look at the breakdown, not the headline number. A model card is documentation as governance: if it doesn't exist, the model doesn't ship."

**Interviewer**: "Walk through the three fairness metrics in `BiasDetector` — what does each measure, and what are the numbers in the demo?"

**Candidate**: "Three different definitions of fairness, deliberately. Demographic parity: the difference in *positive prediction rates* between groups — `|rateA - rateB|`; the demo gets 0.0400, under the 0.1 threshold, meaning both groups are approved at nearly the same rate. Equal opportunity: the difference in *true positive rates* — of the genuinely creditworthy, does the model find them equally in both groups? The demo gets 0.0598, also under 0.1 — the recall gap is small. Disparate impact: the *ratio* of the lower positive rate to the higher — the 80% rule from US employment law; 0.8830 is above 0.8, so it satisfies the rule. The trio matters because they can disagree: you can have demographic parity while one group's *qualified* members are systematically missed — equal opportunity catches that, and the ratio catches severe asymmetry the difference-based metrics can normalize."

**Interviewer**: "The demo simulates two groups with different ground-truth accuracies. What do the numbers tell you about the *model* vs the *data*?"

**Candidate**: "The model was simulated at 92% detection on Group A and 85% on Group B, both against a 30% base rate, and the metrics land comfortably inside the thresholds — DPD 0.040, EOD 0.0598, ratio 0.883. The important distinction: some of the gap is the *model* (different detection rates), some is the *data* — the model card's limitation 'skewed towards urban populations' says Group B is underserved in training, which is a data problem no metric-threshold tweak fixes. The governance action is separate from the metric verdict: mitigation — better data, bias mitigation (the audit trail's last entry is 'Bias mitigation applied') — then re-measure and re-document. The metric is the tripwire, not the fix."

**Interviewer**: "Now the audit trail. How does the hash chain work, and what does `verifyIntegrity` actually prove?"

**Candidate**: "Each `AuditEntry` stores the action, model, version, user, details, timestamp, and the `previousHash` of the entry before it, then computes its own `hash` over *all of that plus the previous hash* — `Integer.toHexString(input.hashCode())`. Because each entry's hash incorporates its predecessor's, the chain is sequential: change any field of any entry and its hash changes, which breaks the next entry's `previousHash` expectation, cascading to the end. `verifyIntegrity` replays the chain from the genesis '0' and compares every recomputed hash against the stored one — any mismatch returns false. What it proves: the log hasn't been silently edited — assuming the stored hashes themselves are outside the attacker's reach (WORM storage, separate service). The lab's `hashCode()` is a 32-bit hash — fine for a demo of the *structure*, but real audit logs need SHA-256 or better, because the collision risk here is real."

**Interviewer**: "Why a hash chain instead of just a signed log file?"

**Candidate**: "A single signature over a file authenticates that file but not its *internal order* — you could reorder entries or drop a suffix and the signature still checks out if you re-sign. The chain makes each entry depend on the one before it, so the log is self-verifying: any deletion, insertion, or reordering is detected by replaying the chain, even without the original signer. That's the property regulators want — non-repudiable history: who promoted what, when, and in what order. The remaining gap: you still need the hashes anchored — periodic signatures by a key held in a safe place, or blockchain-style anchoring — so an attacker can't just recompute and rewrite the whole chain. The lab's `verifyIntegrity` is the check that turns 'trust this log' into 'verify this log'."

**Interviewer**: "The demo logs six events across two model versions. What governance story does that trail tell?"

**Candidate**: "The classic lifecycle: `REGISTER` 1.0.0 by the data scientist, `PROMOTE` to staging and `VALIDATE` by QA, `PROMOTE` to production by the ML engineer — three actors, two gates, all recorded. Then version 2.0.0 registered with 'Updated training data' — the *why* of the retrain — and 2.1.0 promoted with 'Bias mitigation applied', tying the fix to the fairness work in the bias-detection part. So the trail answers the three audit questions: what happened (action), who did it (user), and why (details) — with the version chain making the promotion sequence unambiguous. This is the artifact a regulator's 'show me the history of this model' question resolves to."

**Interviewer**: "How does this lab relate to the model registry in Lab 03 — aren't they the same thing?"

**Candidate**: "They're complementary halves of governance. Lab 03's `ModelRegistry` is the *state*: stages (staging/production), which version is live, promotion logic, rollback. Lab 11's `AuditTrail` is the *history*: every promotion and registration as a tamper-evident record. A registry without a trail can tell you what's deployed but not who decided or why; a trail without a registry can't tell you what's actually serving traffic. Production governance pairs them: registry for the current truth, audit trail for the accountable story — and the model card sits on top as the documentation artifact both feed."

**Interviewer**: "The interview notes mention EU AI Act and GDPR. Where do those touch this lab concretely?"

**Candidate**: "The AI Act pushes exactly the artifacts this lab builds: model documentation (the `ModelCard`), performance-by-group evidence (the per-group metrics), and traceability of decisions (the audit trail) — for high-risk systems like loan approval, which is why the demo model is a loan model. GDPR adds the individual-rights angle: the right to explanation means the *intended use and limitations* must be comprehensible — the card's language matters — and the audit trail supports contestability: if a decision is challenged, the record shows which model version made it. The engineering consequence: governance is not a paperwork step at the end; the card, the metrics, and the chain are code artifacts built *into* the pipeline, reviewed like code."

**Interviewer**: "Walk through how you'd extend the lab to test `verifyIntegrity` properly."

**Candidate**: "Three cases. Happy path: six entries, replay matches, `INTACT`. Tamper path: mutate one entry after the fact — change `user`, `details`, or `version` — recompute nothing, and `verifyIntegrity` must return false; because hashes chain, even mutating the *last* entry breaks the check. Structural path: drop an entry from the middle and verify the chain no longer links — the following entry's `previousHash` won't match. I'd also assert the hash-change property: any field change changes `entry.hash` (except in the pathological 32-bit collision case, which is itself the argument for SHA-256 in production). Those three assertions are exactly what I'd wire into CI, mirroring the gate discipline from Lab 09 — governance code needs its own validation."

**Interviewer**: "One more: the fairness metrics use `abs` differences and thresholds of 0.1. What's wrong with a fixed threshold?"

**Candidate**: "A single global threshold hides two problems. Statistically, small-sample groups have noisy rates — the demo's `sample_size: 1000.0` per group is generous; with 100 samples the DPD of 0.04 could easily fluctuate across a 0.1 threshold, so the threshold must come with confidence intervals or the check is a coin flip. Contextually, fairness is domain-dependent: a 0.04 difference that's fine for loan approval may be unacceptable in credit scoring or hiring; regulators often set their own rules (the 80% rule is itself a legal threshold, not a statistical one). The right design: report the metric *and* its confidence interval, set thresholds per domain with sign-off, and re-check on every re-training — which is what the walkthrough's flow does: metrics computed from seeded simulation, thresholds evaluated, and the verdict written into the model card's `fairnessMetrics`."

**Interviewer**: "Finally — the lab's Q3: bias detected in production. What's your response plan?"

**Candidate**: "The playbook: 1) verify — confirm the metric isn't an artifact of a data pipeline change, using the per-group sample sizes and confidence intervals; 2) triage severity — the demo's ratio 0.883 is above 0.8, so it's a monitoring flag, not a mandatory pause; a ratio under 0.8 or an EOD over 0.1 would escalate; 3) fix the data or the model — enrich the under-represented group's training data, or apply bias mitigation (the audit trail records it as `PROMOTE 2.1.0 'Bias mitigation applied'`); 4) re-measure and re-document — new metrics into the model card, new version in the registry (Lab 03), new audit entries; 5) communicate — the limitation section gets updated, regulators get the report. The governance rule underneath: bias detection is not a one-time check at release — it's a monitoring signal, exactly like the drift detector in Lab 08, because models drift in fairness the same way they drift in accuracy."
