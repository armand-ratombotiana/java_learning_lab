# Lab 14: Mock Interview — AutoML Pipelines

**Role**: ML Platform Engineer / Applied ML Engineer
**Duration**: 60 minutes
**Focus**: Hyperparameter spaces and scales, grid vs random vs Bayesian search, simulated training evaluation, reproducibility of search, AutoML in the training pipeline

---

**Interviewer**: "Start with the space definition. What does the lab's `HyperparameterSpace` model, and why does scale matter so much?"

**Candidate**: "It's a tiny but honest model of a search space: `addCategorical` for choices like the optimizer, `addNumerical` for ranges with an explicit scale — `"log"` or `"linear"`. The scale is the key detail: learning rates span orders of magnitude (0.0001 to 0.1), so uniform linear sampling would waste trials in the sparse high-lr region and starve the promising low-lr region — log scale makes `0.0001, 0.0006, 0.0032, 0.0178, 0.1` the grid's five values, which are evenly spaced in log space. Getting the scale wrong is the most common hyperparameter-search mistake in production, and the lab forces you to declare it per parameter. Every sampler — grid, random, Bayesian perturbation — then respects the declared scale, which is the right architecture: one space definition, many search strategies over it."

**Interviewer**: "Walk through the three strategies and their results: grid 0.9335 in 375 trials, random 0.9313 in 30, Bayesian 0.9353 in 30."

**Candidate**: "Grid search is exhaustive over a lattice — 5 values per numeric parameter times 3 optimizers = 375 combinations — and it finds a strong 0.9335 because the lattice happens to include a point near the learning-rate sweet spot (0.0032 vs the surface optimum near 0.003). But 375 trials for five values per axis is the lattice's curse: coverage is dense in name and sparse in reality. Random search samples 30 continuous points and gets 0.9313 — within two-tenths of a point of the grid with 12× fewer trials, because every random point has unique values on every axis. Bayesian optimization spends 10 trials uniformly, then 20 trials perturbing the running best — Gaussian steps of 10% of each numeric range, clamped — and lands at 0.9353, the winner, in the same 30 trials as random. The demo's point: strategy choice is worth more than trial count."

**Interviewer**: "The lab's Bayesian optimization is 'simplified' — it perturbs the best instead of fitting a surrogate. What's the real thing, and what does the simplification cost?"

**Candidate**: "Real Bayesian optimization maintains a probabilistic surrogate of the objective — typically a Gaussian process or TPE model — and an acquisition function (expected improvement, UCB) that balances exploring uncertain regions against exploiting the predicted optimum; each trial updates the surrogate. The lab's version keeps the *explore-then-exploit shape* — uniform warm-up, then local refinement — but replaces the model with a random perturbation of the incumbent. What it costs: no uncertainty-aware exploration (it can't jump to a promising-but-unvisited region), no explicit exploration-exploitation tradeoff, and no reuse of prior trial information beyond the single best. What it buys: determinism, no library dependencies, and a 40-line implementation that demonstrates the loop. For a production AutoML service I'd bring in a real library, but the lab's structure — warm-up, then refine near the best — is the correct mental model."

**Interviewer**: "`simulateTraining` encodes the search surface. Walk through what it models and why the walkthrough had to fix it."

**Candidate**: "The simulator is a smooth accuracy surface: a quadratic penalty on `log10(lr)` peaking near 0.003, a quadratic on `log(batch_size)` peaking near 90, `log(hiddenUnits)` diminishing returns, a dropout term favoring ~0.3, and optimizer offsets — adam +0.02, rmsprop +0.01, sgd -0.01 — plus a small Gaussian noise draw. That surface is what makes the search meaningful: the optimizers are rewarded for finding the 0.003-lr, ~90-batch region. The fix: the lab reads `batch_size` with a raw `(int)` cast, but `gridCombinations` emits rounded `Double`s — the very first grid trial throws `ClassCastException`. The walkthrough reads it through `((Number) ...).intValue()`, which accepts both the grid's Double and the Integer default. It's the same type-boundary lesson as the feature store's Number casts: a heterogeneous map needs a single numeric conversion point."

**Interviewer**: "Reproducibility: the lab uses `new Random(42)` in three different places. Is the demo actually deterministic?"

**Candidate**: "Yes — but only by luck of design, and the walkthrough preserves each decision deliberately. Grid search shares one sequential `Random(42)` across all 375 trials, so the noise sequence is fixed. Random search samples the space with the seeded `rng` in submission order, and each trial's noise uses its own `new Random(42)` — a fixed noise draw per trial regardless of which of the four pool threads runs it, which is what keeps the result deterministic under thread scheduling. Bayesian uses the sequential `Random(42)` throughout. The general rule this teaches: seeded per-thread RNGs, never shared across threads, and the seed itself recorded as a hyperparameter of the run — the walkthrough's Problem 3 spells out how that extends to nightly production searches."

**Interviewer**: "Why does the best-of-grid print `{optimizer=adam, learning_rate=0.0032, ...}` — optimizer first? And what does it tell you about `LinkedHashMap` order?"

**Candidate**: "The order is an artifact of `gridCombinations`: it iterates `categorical.forEach` first, then `numerical.forEach`, so the cartesian product's key order is optimizer, learning_rate, batch_size, dropout — not the insertion order of the space definition. The printed map is the key-by-key order of the deepest copy in `cartesianProduct`, and every combination shares it, which is exactly why the transcript is stable. The lesson: map iteration order is part of your output contract — for logs, tests, and golden transcripts, use `LinkedHashMap` deliberately and know which insertion sequence your code produces; a `HashMap` here would randomize the printed params and break any diff-based comparison."

**Interviewer**: "Tie AutoML into the MLOps stack — where does search live in the pipeline?"

**Candidate**: "Search is the upstream half of training: the Lab 07 pipeline stages it before training — typically an `auto-tune` job that runs the searches, records every trial to Lab 02's experiment tracking, and passes the winning configuration to the training stage. The governance hooks matter: the winning params belong in the model card (Lab 11) with the search seed, the evaluation of the winner runs through the validation gate (Lab 09), and the winner's artifact goes to the registry (Lab 03). Without those hooks, AutoML produces an untraceable model — a card that says 'found by search' but can't reproduce the search is the AutoML version of a data leak."

**Interviewer**: "What are the failure modes of AutoML in production that this lab doesn't show?"

**Candidate**: "Four. Data leakage: the simulator has no train/test split, but real search must score on a validation split with a fixed partition, or the search 'finds' the test set — the simulator's smooth surface hides the possibility, which makes it more dangerous. Trial heterogeneity: real trials consume varying GPU time, so a 30-trial budget is a wall-clock bet; the lab's fixed durations hide stragglers (the Lab 13 lesson). Nonstationarity: if the data distribution drifts between trials — Lab 08 territory — the best-so-far is stale, and search must re-evaluate baselines; the simulator's static surface can't show that. And cost control: 375 grid trials on real GPUs is a day of cluster time; the demo's cheapness is the trap — always estimate trial cost × trials before launching a search."

**Interviewer**: "How would you CI-test the search code?"

**Candidate**: "Three tiers, mirroring the other labs. Golden transcript: the run output — best per strategy and the summary — must equal the captured Expected Output, so any change to the space, the simulator, or the RNG usage is a visible diff. Structural: gridCombinations of a known space yields exactly 375 maps, all with the same key set, all values within declared bounds, and all rounded to 4 decimals; randomSample honors the scale (log samples are in log space); the cartesian product has no duplicates. Behavioral: on the known surface, Bayesian's 30-trial best is >= random's 30-trial best *in expectation over seeds* — with the fixed seed it's a strict inequality (0.9353 > 0.9313), which the test can assert directly. Those three tiers are the same discipline as every other lab: type the boundary, freeze the transcript, assert the behavior."
